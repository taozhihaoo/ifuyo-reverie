The Long Walk: Notes From Sixty Rest Stops

I started walking the old canal path on the first of March, carrying nothing but a notebook and a thermos. What follows is what I wrote at each rest stop, lightly edited for this essay.

Rest stop three taught me the first lesson: distances lie. The map said four miles; my feet said seven. I recalibrated my trust accordingly and bought better socks at the next village shop.

The towpath at stop five was lined with poplars that had been planted, according to a faded board, by prisoners of a war nobody local could name.

At stop six a lock keeper waved me through without a boat in sight, apparently just to keep the mechanism honest.

Stop eight smelled of tar and wet rope. I wrote nothing for an hour and the notebook is better for it.

By rest stop ten the blisters had stopped asking for attention and started negotiating. We settled on moleskin and reduced daily mileage.

Stop thirteen had a pub that sold tea in beer glasses, which turned out to be the correct way to serve tea on a walking day.

A heron stood at stop fifteen with the patience of a statue and the appetite of a landlord.

Rest stop seventeen was a bench with a plaque for a man who apparently also liked benches. I shook hands with the armrest.

The rain at stop nineteen lasted exactly as long as it took me to unpack the rain jacket, then reconsidered.

Stop twenty-one crossed an aqueduct. The canal runs over a road which runs over a river, and everybody pretends this is normal.

At rest stop twenty-four I met a dog who considered my thermos a personal insult. His owner apologized twice; the dog did not.

Stop twenty-six was closed for repairs, which for a bench means it was simply absent.

The village store at stop twenty-eight sold postcards of itself, which is either vanity or healthy self-esteem.

Rest stop thirty-one smelled of rain that had not yet decided to fall.

At stop thirty-three I gave directions to a cyclist, twice, both times wrong, with great confidence.

Stop thirty-five had cow traffic. The herd crossed with the unhurried authority of a railway timetable.

By rest stop forty I had stopped counting miles and started counting herons: eleven so far.

Stop forty-two was a bridge with initials carved so old the tree had grown around them into lumps, like scars keeping secrets.

The inn at stop forty-four charged fair prices for fair soup. I stayed two nights and lied to myself that it was weather.

Stop forty-seven smelled of cut hay and diesel, the two crops of the modern valley.

Rest stop fifty-two offered the best view of the trip and the worst sandwich. Both are remembered; only one is forgiven.

At stop fifty-five I met the only other long-distance walker of the trip. We compared blisters like medals and parted like friends.

Stop fifty-seven was nothing at all: a stretch of grass, a bent fence, a memory of a milestone.

At rest stop sixty the canal met the river, and the walk simply ended, the way good walks do, without announcing anything.

If you ask whether I would do it again: the question answers itself every morning I am not on the path.
